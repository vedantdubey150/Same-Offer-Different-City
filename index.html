The question. Indian women students enrol and perform at least as well as men, yet they are less likely to take internships outside their home city. This site shows the gap, why standard economic theory can't explain it, and a model in which everyone is rational and the gap still appears.

01 · The question

The gap is real

Women are as qualified as men and apply just as often. The gap opens when it's time to actually move to another city.

Four costs of moving

Rent

Girls' PGs near Gurgaon list at ₹8,000–₹20,000 a month. Even the cheapest shared bed takes the whole ₹5,000 PMIS stipend, and traditional PGs set 10 or 11 PM curfews for women.

Commute

Indian women work closer to home and travel more slowly: 37% of urban women walk to work, against 27% of men.

Safety

Delhi University women accept colleges about 8.5 ranks lower to get a safer route, and would pay ₹7,500 a year more than men for one. Men barely trade at all.

Family

Only 42% of women aged 15–49 may go alone to the market, a health facility and outside their community (NFHS-5). Households spend about 21% less on daughters' education.

Why it matters

Internships pay off

An internship raises early-career earnings by about 6% (Germany) and interview invitations by 12.6% (Belgium). A woman who can't take one starts her career behind.

India's missing women workers

Only 27.7% of urban women aged 15+ are in the labour force, against 76.2% of men; among urban 15–29-year-olds it's 23.2% vs 60.9%. A first internship is often the bridge to a first job.

02 · Standard theory

Why the textbook falls short

Standard models say an internship is an investment: same payoff and same price, same decision. The evidence says otherwise.

Model A

Human capital theory

Becker and Mincer. Take the internship if the future earnings gain plus the stipend beats rent, travel, setup and what you give up.

Model B

Job search and compensating differentials

Rosen. Accept if the stipend minus location costs beats your best alternative. Distance is a price.

Model C

Unitary household

Becker; Samuelson. The family pools income and funds whichever child has the higher net return, son or daughter.

Same ability + same returns + same prices  ⇒  same uptake

All three models give the same prediction. Since women are at least as well qualified, human capital theory says they should take at least as many internships as men.

What contradicts it

The gap opens at joining

PMIS: women's share falls from 41% of Round 2 applicants to about 31% of joinees. If women apply at 41%, the model says they should join at 41%.

It widens with distance

DDU-GKY women were even less likely to accept jobs that require migration. Once placed, they stayed as long as or longer than men, so they aren't less committed.

Distance costs women more

Women value commute about 20% more than men (France). Delhi women would pay ₹7,500 a year more than men for a safer route.

When the price falls, women move

Foxconn's state-built hostel houses 18,720 women workers. Tata Electronics' Hosur plant employs 15,000+ women with free lodging on campus. Odisha women recruited in groups had twice the placement success.

The two assumptions that fail

1. Same prices

A woman pays the same rent and travel as a man, plus a safety cost: a safer, dearer room, cabs after dark, curfews. In our survey, 84% of women vs 30% of men say they'd need extra money each month to feel safe.

2. One neutral family

Moving needs parents' permission and often their money, and families don't weigh sons and daughters the same. Only 36% of women vs 79% of men in our survey say their parents would let them live alone.

03 · The model

Same payoff, higher price

Why rational families send fewer daughters to out-of-city internships.

A student, her parents and an employer each make the best choice they can. Women and men get the same payoff from an internship and face the same rent and stipend. Two things differ. A woman pays an extra safety cost to live and travel in an unfamiliar city, and her parents bear a norm cost (worry, reputation, what relatives will say) when a daughter lives alone.

Both raise the cutoff an internship must clear before the family says yes. With the same spread of payoffs, a higher cutoff means fewer women go. Nobody is irrational and no employer discriminates, yet a gap appears: in the worked example, 87.5% of men and 58.75% of women go. A safe employer hostel cuts the gap from 28.75 to about 10.6 points; a flat stipend rise doesn't shrink it at all.

1. Agents, choices and objectives

AgentChoosesMaximises
StudentAccept the out-of-city internship, or stay with the local or work-from-home option (valued at zero)Her own net benefit: R + W − H − T − S
ParentsPermit or refuse, and whether to pay the shortfallFamily welfare: the child's net benefit minus their norm cost, R + W − H − T − S − N
EmployerStipend, and whether to offer a safe hostel (cost k per intern housed)Profit: (value of an intern v − W) × interns who join, minus k × interns housed

Why parents hold the deciding vote

Indian undergraduates usually need permission to live away, and parents often pay the gap between stipend and costs. So the family is not one neutral maximiser: the daughter's payoff counts, but parents also weigh a cost that falls only on them, and only for daughters.

Why everyone is rational

The student weighs real costs she faces. Parents weigh real worry and real social costs, which shape decisions even when partly misperceived (Bursztyn et al.). The employer pays for housing only when it raises profit.

2. The two features that differ by gender

A safety tax on her

Safety cost S

For a woman, the same rent and distance buy less. She picks a PG in a safer area, takes a cab after dark instead of the metro, avoids cheaper but poorly lit routes, and accepts curfews that cut into work hours.

  • Delhi University women would pay about ₹7,500 a year more than men for a safer route, and pick a much lower-ranked college to get it (Borker, 2021).
  • Women value commute about 20% more than men (Le Barbanchon, Rathelot and Roulet).
  • Traditional women's PGs commonly set 10 or 11 PM curfews.
A norm tax on her parents

Norm cost N

Parents value a daughter's future earnings as much as a son's. But a daughter living alone brings worry about her safety, questions from relatives, and fears for her reputation and marriage prospects.

  • Only 42% of women aged 15–49 may go alone to the market, a health facility and outside their community (NFHS-5).
  • DDU-GKY women most often turn down offers for personal, family and migration reasons; men for low pay.
  • Households spend about 21% less on daughters' education (NSS 75th round).
  • Mobility restrictions and concern for female "purity" persist as incomes rise (Jayachandran).

Why only these two? They match the two assumptions that failed, and each has its own evidence and its own policy lever: S responds to safe housing and transport; N responds to information, cohort placements and trusted institutions.

3. Step by step: how rational choices create the gap

  1. The student's choice. She wants to go if her net benefit beats the local option.
    R + W − H − T − S > 0
  2. The parents' choice. They permit, and fund any shortfall, only if the family's net benefit is positive. That adds their norm cost.
    R + W − H − T − S − N > 0
  3. The cutoff. Rearranging, the internship happens only if its payoff R beats a cutoff C. For a son, S and N are about zero; for a daughter the cutoff is higher by S + N.
    C = (H + T − W) + S + N
  4. From cutoffs to shares. Payoffs vary across students, but the spread is the same for women and men. The share who go is the share whose payoff lies above their cutoff, so a higher cutoff means a smaller share of women.
  5. The gap. With equal payoffs, the gap comes entirely from S + N. Remove both and it disappears, which is exactly the standard model's prediction.
  6. Why applications look fine but joining doesn't. Applying costs a few minutes, and parents usually weigh in only once there's a concrete offer in a named city. So the gap is small at application and opens at acceptance.
  7. The employer. A firm offers a safe hostel if the extra interns it attracts are worth more than the hostel costs. A hostel also has a fixed setup cost, so only firms hiring many women at one site find it pays. Each firm is rational, but most women interns are left facing the full S + N.
    extra joiners × (v − W) > k × interns housed

Safety or family?

Of women's extra ₹23,000 cutoff, ₹8,000 (35%) is safety and ₹15,000 (65%) is the parents' norm cost. Without N, women's share would be 77.5%: safety alone produces a 10-point gap.

S 35%N 65%

Who goes: selection

Average payoff of those who go = (cutoff + ₹80,000) ÷ 2: ₹45,000 for men, ₹56,500 for women. The women who go hold internships worth about 26% more. They cleared a higher bar, so they should perform as well or better, and stay as long or longer.

Extensions

Bargaining power

Give the daughter a smaller say in the family decision than a son. This works like a higher N.

Patrilocality

If parents expect a daughter's later earnings to go to her marital household, they weight her R less, lowering the family's value of her internship.

Misperceived norms

Split N into a real part and a misperceived part. Information campaigns remove only the misperceived part.

Companion note

The model on one page

Question. Why do women undergraduates in India take out-of-city internships less often than men with the same ability?

Agents and objectives. A student chooses between an out-of-city internship and a local or work-from-home option (valued at zero) to maximise her net benefit R + W − H − T − S. Her parents, who must permit the move and often fund it, maximise family welfare: her net benefit minus their norm cost N. An employer chooses whether to provide a safe hostel to maximise profit: (v − W) × joiners − k × interns housed.

Symbols. R = payoff from the internship (same spread for women and men); W = stipend; H = rent; T = travel and setup; S = safety cost (positive for women, about zero for men); N = parents' norm cost (positive for daughters, zero for sons).

Decision rule. The internship happens if R > C, where C = (H + T − W) + S + N.

Result. Since S + N is positive for women and about zero for men, women face a higher cutoff. With the same spread of R, a smaller share of women clear it. Ability, returns, rent and stipend are identical, so the gap comes only from S + N.

Policy. Lower S (safe hostels, transport, relocation allowances) and N (cohort placements, parent information, trusted institutions). A safe hostel alone cuts the example gap from 28.75 to 10.6 points; a ₹4,000-a-month stipend rise leaves it at 28.75.
