Same CGPA. Same offer. Only one of them goes.

Ananya and Arjun both got a two-month internship at a Bengaluru startup. This site asks why women students are less likely to take internships in another city. Click the glowing things in the room, or scroll to fly out of the window.

The question. Among undergraduates in Indian cities who get an in-office internship offer in another city, women accept less often than men with the same grades, course and offer. Here is the evidence, why the textbook can't explain it, and a model where everyone is rational and the gap still appears.

01 · The question · 20%

The gap is real

No national dataset records out-of-city internships by gender, so the case rests on four kinds of evidence that all point the same way. The survey in the notes measures the gap directly.

Welfare

In a randomised résumé test, internship experience raised interview requests by about 14%. A declined internship is a lost step up.

Efficiency

Women are about 48% of students in higher education. If they can't follow the best match across cities, talent lands in the wrong jobs.

In the PM Internship Scheme pilot, location was a leading reason offers were turned down. The parliamentary committee flagged that relocation and safety problems may hit women harder.

02 · Standard theory · 25%

What the textbook predicts

Human capital theory, with a unitary household. An internship is an investment. Take it if the career gain is bigger than the extra cost:

Go to city O  ⇔  ΔR ≥ C − S

ΔR is the career gain, C the cost of living there, S the stipend. A unitary household pools its money and backs whichever child has the higher return.

Prediction: same ability, same rents, same tastes, so the same take-up for women and men.

Where the evidence breaks it.

The gap shows up after an offer is made, and it grows with distance. The textbook can only explain that if moving costs rise faster with distance for women. That is a gendered cost, which it assumes away.

Women are not avoiding investing in themselves: female enrolment in higher education has been above male for five years in a row.

Ruling out the easy answers

Easy explanationWhy it doesn't fit
Women have less education or skillFemale gross enrolment is above male (gender parity index 1.01). The survey holds CGPA constant.
Women are less productive, so returns are lowerOnce placed, women stayed in jobs as long as or longer than men.
Women don't want to workThe gap is about moving. Women take internships that need no move, such as remote ones.
Employers only hire menThe gap is in accepting an offer, after the employer has already said yes.

What's left: the constraints and payoffs around moving differ by gender. Play it and see.

03 · Rational-choice model · 35%

The game, written out

Moving costs a daughter more, and the parents who have to cover the shortfall get back less from her career. Everyone optimises, and nobody's ability or preferences differ.

AgentChoosesMaximises
Student (woman or man)Accept the out-of-city offer O, or stay with the local option LCareer gain ΔR minus own costs, including harassment risk h
ParentsPay the shortfall and give permission (both needed)Their share θ of the child's gain, minus the money they pay, minus reputational cost σ
Employer (extension)Whom to make out-of-city offers toValue of the hire minus recruiting cost, given the chance the offer is accepted

Same for both

Career gain ΔR is spread evenly from ₹0 to ₹1,00,000. Stipend S = ₹16,000 for two months, matching the 2024 average of about ₹8,000 a month. A 20-year-old can't borrow, so any shortfall D = C − S must come from the parents.

Feature 1: the price of being safe

The same level of safety costs a woman more: a women's PG, a bigger deposit, a cab instead of the metro after dark. So her cost is the man's cost plus a premium π, and she also bears a harassment-risk cost h.

Feature 2: what parents get back

Under patrilocal marriage, a daughter's later earnings mostly go to her husband's household, so θ is lower for daughters. Parents of a daughter living alone also face "log kya kahenge", a reputational cost σ.

Decision rules

Student wants to go:  ΔR ≥ D + h
Parents agree:  θ·ΔR − σ ≥ D  ⇔  ΔR ≥ (D + σ) / θ

She goes only if both hold. Because θ is below 1, the parents' threshold is the one that binds, so take-up = 1 − threshold / 100 (in ₹ thousand).

The two features multiply

The safety cost alone opens a 20-point gap, and the family effect alone opens 14. Together they open 39, because every rupee of extra cost is divided by a smaller θ.

Employers make it stick

Recruiters who see women accepting less often rationally make them fewer out-of-city offers. That is statistical discrimination, and it keeps families from ever seeing a daughter go.

What the model predicts beyond the gap

Relatives nearby

A relative in the internship city cuts rent and σ, so the gap shrinks.

No gap if remote

With no move, D, h and σ are zero, so women take remote internships as often as men.

Women who go are stronger

Only women with ΔR above 55 go, against 16 for men, so women who do move should outperform and convert more pre-placement offers.

Longer means wider

Rent grows every month and the gain grows more slowly, so 12-month placements show the biggest gaps.

Model note, survey and sources

The model on one card

Student g goes iff ΔR ≥ Dg + hg and θgΔR − σg ≥ Dg, with Dg = Cg − S. Gendered features: CF = CM + π, hF > 0 (safety); θF < θM, σF > 0 (family). With ΔR ~ U[0, 100], take-up = 1 − (Dg + σg)/(100·θg). Example: men 84%, women 45%.

The survey

60 to 100 second- and third-year students, about half women, anonymous. It asks about offers, acceptance and reasons for declining, the monthly cost of a place they'd feel safe in, what family would pay, whether they'd need permission, parents' worry about relatives (1 to 5), and CGPA and course. The analysis compares acceptance by gender within the same CGPA band and course.
